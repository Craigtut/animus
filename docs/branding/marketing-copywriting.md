# Marketing Copywriting

> **Operational guide.** This is the working reference for outward-facing copy: landing pages, blog posts, social, release notes, store listings, email, anything a prospective person reads. It is downstream of the mastheads. `brand-vision.md` owns the voice; `product-vision.md` owns what we are. When this guide and a masthead disagree, the masthead wins. For in-product UI text, see `product-copywriting.md`.

---

## How to use this guide (read first)

This guide deliberately avoids a library of approved phrases. A phrase bank becomes a crutch: the same lines get reused until they are stale, and copy stops being written and starts being assembled. Instead this teaches the **reasoning and the tests**, so every new piece is written fresh from principle.

Where an example appears, it is there to illustrate a *way of thinking*, not to be copied. Treat every example as disposable. If you find yourself reaching for a line because you saw it here, stop and write your own from the principle behind it.

## The governing law

**Meaning is the subject. Features are evidence, never the subject.**

Almost every weak draft fails the same way: it takes a capability as its subject and dresses it in nicer language. "It has memory, here is the poetic version of memory" is still feature copy wearing a sweater. The meaning layer is not a prettier description of a feature. It is what the thing *means*: that something is given room to become a particular character, with a point of view and taste of its own, that you come to know over time.

A capability earns a place in the copy only when it is load-bearing proof of a meaning, and even then it is the evidence, not the headline. Lead with what it means. Reach for a feature only when the meaning needs proof.

## The register

The default voice is **grounded.** We describe a simulated entity that becomes someone, plainly and with quiet conviction. We do not announce that it is alive.

We do not use, ever:

- "It's alive." Artificial life. "Artificial life, not artificial intelligence." An experiment in artificial consciousness. "It was thinking before you got here." These overclaim, they sound like slogans, and they are the opposite of the grounded confidence we want. They are retired.

Provocation is allowed, but only as an occasional tool, never the constant drumbeat. A single bold, strange line lands hard precisely because the writing around it is calm and grounded. If every line is provocative, none of them are.

## Principles

These are adapted for Animus. Read them as lenses, not rules to recite.

1. **The product is the marketing.** When the thing carries a real point of view, the most compelling move is to describe it plainly. State what it is; the fact does the work. When tempted to add a superlative, remove it.
2. **Meaning over mechanics.** Write what something means for a person, not what it does technically. Mechanics live in documentation. Copy is for meaning.
3. **Specificity at the meaning layer.** Precision beats polish, but aim the precision at the *entity's emergent inner life and what it becomes*, not at system mechanics. A specific, surprising detail about a thought it had or an opinion it formed carries weight. A specific detail about tick intervals or databases does not. Be concretely specific about meaning, in a plain, unhurried way.
4. **Concept before product.** Lead with the idea and what it means; let the download be the quiet resolution at the end, not the opening pitch. Why it exists, then what it means, then how to get it.
5. **Confident invitation, never urgency.** No scarcity, no countdowns, no "don't miss out." Present Animus as something that exists whether or not anyone engages. This reads as warmth, not coldness: there is generosity in not needing to convince anyone. Calls to action are invitations, never demands.
6. **People and a relationship, not customers.** Never "users," "customers," or "subscribers." But lean on the *relational* framing (something you keep, something you live alongside) rather than a movement or hype framing. We are not recruiting members of a movement; we are describing a being someone keeps close.
7. **Discovery over explanation.** Do not explain everything. Describe the experience and leave room for people to discover the rest. The strongest proof is showing a real, surprising artifact of the entity's inner life rather than claiming it is surprising. Show, do not assert.
8. **Critique by contrast, lightly.** The best commentary on hollow software is a thing that is not hollow. State the status quo briefly and factually if at all, then describe what Animus is and let the reader draw the conclusion. Do not attack incumbents; we define ourselves by what we are, not against others.
9. **Features are evidence, never the subject.** (The governing law, restated as a principle because it is the one most often broken.) If a capability appears, it is in service of a meaning. If you cannot name the meaning a feature serves, leave the feature out.
10. **Honesty.** Be direct about what works, what does not yet, what costs money and why, and what the intentions are. Trust that survives mistakes is worth more than initial enthusiasm.

## On the word "assistant"

We do not ban "assistant." Animus genuinely can assist, and pretending otherwise is its own kind of dishonesty. What we avoid is *reducing* it to "an AI assistant," the generic category line that could sit on any product's page. Acknowledging that it helps is fine when meaning leads. Defining it as an assistant in the hero is not. Lead with what it means; let usefulness be the byproduct, the way the positioning says.

## Craft and structure

These are safe to apply directly; they shape rhythm without seeding reusable phrases.

- **Never use em dashes.** Commas, colons, semicolons, parentheses, or separate sentences.
- **Short sentences carry the weight.** The most important claim should usually be the shortest line.
- **Fragments are fine** when deliberate. They create rhythm and emphasis. Use them on purpose, not from laziness.
- **Lead with the strongest line.** Open every section and paragraph with its most compelling sentence. Do not build up to the point; state it, then support it.
- **One idea per paragraph** in long-form.
- **Sentence case** for headlines and labels. "Animus" is always capitalized.
- **Periods over exclamation marks.** Exclamation marks undermine quiet confidence; avoid them in headlines and CTAs entirely.
- **Oxford comma, always.**

## What we do not say

Enumerating these is safe, because banning words does not seed reuse.

- **The retired life-claims** above ("it's alive," "artificial life," etc.).
- **Empty superlatives:** revolutionary, groundbreaking, cutting-edge, next-generation, best-in-class, game-changing.
- **AI marketing clichés:** smart, intelligent, powerful (as adjectives for Animus), supercharge, unlock, unleash, seamless, frictionless, leverage, optimize.
- **Urgency and scarcity:** limited time, don't miss out, act now, exclusive (as pressure).
- **Corporate buzzwords:** solution, platform, ecosystem (as marketing terms), scalable, robust, enterprise-grade.
- **Delegation framing:** "on your behalf," "manages your..." The relationship is parallel, not a hand-off. It exists alongside you.
- **"AI" as the category label** for Animus. Describe what it is and what it means rather than filing it under a category.

## Tests to run before a line ships

Ask, in order:

1. **Subject test.** Is the subject of this sentence a *meaning*, or a *feature*? If a feature, rewrite so the meaning is the subject and the feature is at most the evidence.
2. **Anyone test.** Could this exact line appear on any other AI product's page? If yes, it is not ours yet.
3. **Claim-or-show test.** Am I *claiming* something is interesting, or *showing* something that is? Prefer showing.
4. **Performance test.** Am I stating plainly, or performing (hype, urgency, superlatives)? Strip the performance.
5. **Strongest-line test.** Is the most compelling sentence first?

## The few fixed phrases

We keep almost nothing fixed, on purpose. The one anchor worth consistency is the positioning from `product-vision.md`: *something that becomes, not a tool you run*, and its companion, *its usefulness is a byproduct of who it becomes.* Beyond that, write fresh every time.

## Anti-patterns

- **Meaning-flavored feature copy.** A feature dressed in poetic language is still feature copy. Check the subject.
- **Provocation without substance.** A bold line must rest on something real. Never claim what the design cannot support.
- **Nihilism.** Any critique of the status quo must imply that things could be better. The tone is "we wanted something different," never "everything is broken."
- **Jargon disguised as specificity.** Specific means a concrete, human detail, not insider terminology.
- **Performed indifference.** Confident invitation is warm. "Here is what we made, we think it is worth keeping" is right. "We do not care if you use it" is not.
- **Phrase reuse.** If a line here or in past copy starts showing up everywhere, it has become a crutch. Retire it and write fresh.
